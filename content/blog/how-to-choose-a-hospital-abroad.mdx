Choose a hospital abroad by the written answers to ten questions, not by a photograph of a lobby. Ask who operates, which accreditation the campus holds, what the quote includes, how complications are billed, and who sees you after you fly home. If those answers are missing, you do not have a plan yet. TechDr collects them from partner hospitals before you are asked to pay.

## Why do the questions matter more than the building?

Hospitals market buildings because buildings photograph well. You are buying a team, a device if you need one, a bed with a known level of care, and a route home. Two accredited hospitals can both be reasonable. The better one for you is the one whose letter matches your scans and whose exclusions you can live with. The questions below are the ones we ask. You should ask them too, even if a coordinator is in the middle.

## 1. Who will perform the procedure?

You want the role and seniority of the person who will operate or who will be in the room, not a brand name on a website. For major surgery, ask whether a senior specialist is the operator. Training cases happen in good hospitals. You can accept a supervised trainee, or you can ask for the senior. You cannot make that choice if nobody tells you.

Also ask who the anaesthetist is in organisational terms: a consultant-led service, and a plan if your heart or airway is difficult. Anaesthesia is half of surgical safety.

## 2. Is the hospital accredited, and can you verify it?

Look for NABH, India’s national hospital accreditation, or JCI, the international one, and check the accreditor’s public directory. A logo on a brochure is not the check. Accreditation covers systems. It is still worth having, because infection control, consent, and medication safety are systems. It is not a personal success rate.

If a hospital has no accreditation and asks you to trust a tour instead, treat that as a reason to slow down, not as a charming local detail.

## 3. What exactly is included in the price?

Read the line items. Surgery, anaesthesia, the device or implant, ward nights, a named ICU allowance, standard tests, and standard medicines should each be marked in or out. Airport pickup, a hotel, and an interpreter are service items. They are useful. They are not a substitute for the clinical lines. A single round number with no list is not a quote.

Compare the list with the procedure page for your operation so you know the usual stay. A quote for three nights after a major joint, with no explanation, deserves a question.

## 4. What is excluded, and what does an extra day cost?

Exclusions are where surprise bills live. Ask about extra ICU days, a return to theatre, blood transfusion, a more expensive implant, treatment of an unrelated illness, and the companion’s bed. Ask for the price of one extra ward day and one extra ICU day. A team that cannot quote those numbers has not priced the operation.

Also ask what happens if you are not fit on the day. A refund rule for a cancelled case belongs in the same letter as the price.

## 5. Does the plan match your reports?

A hospital that offers bypass before anyone has seen your angiogram is selling a package, not a plan. Send the films. Ask what the team would do if the films show a different diagnosis. The right answer is sometimes “you do not need to travel”. A coordinator who is disappointed by that answer is the wrong coordinator.

Bring your own questions in writing. Ask the hospital to answer in writing. Spoken reassurance in a corridor is hard to rely on later.

## 6. How are complications handled in the first week?

Ask which doctor you call at night, where you go from the hotel, and whether a complication in the first admission is financially capped. Ask how infections are treated and whether a second operation is a new package. You are not asking them to promise there will be no complication. You are asking them to show they have a route.

If you cannot name the night number before you fly, you are not ready to fly.

## 7. Can you consent in a language you understand?

Consent is a conversation. If you need Arabic, French, Russian, Bengali, Hindi, or another language, arrange the interpreter before admission, not during the signature. Ask for a copy of what you signed. Refuse to sign a blank consent or a form you have not read in a language you understand.

The same rule applies to the price. A number you nodded at is not a contract.

## 8. What will your doctor at home receive?

Ask for the operation note, device stickers, a medicine list with generic names, and the follow-up plan. Ask whether those papers will be in English. Your doctor at home has to continue blood thinners, antibiotics, or immunosuppression. A missing paper is a clinical problem, not an administrative one.

Ask for the papers before the discharge day becomes a rush to the airport.

## 9. Is the city practical for your flight and your recovery?

Delhi NCR, Mumbai, Chennai, Hyderabad, Bengaluru, and Kolkata are the cities TechDr uses for most plans. The practical test is a sensible flight from your country, a hotel near the campus, and a stay long enough for the operation you are having. A city that is fashionable but requires two connections and a three-hour transfer is a worse medical choice than a plainer campus with a direct flight.

## 10. Who is accountable after you land at home?

Ask who answers a wound photo on day ten, and how quickly. Ask your own doctor, before you leave home, whether they will see you on return. Medical travel fails when everyone is responsible in the brochure and nobody is responsible on a Sunday. TechDr stays on the case to pass questions to the treating team. Put the ten answers in one page. If three of them are missing, do not pay a large deposit. Compare the price line with the 2026 guide only after the list is complete. A knee quoted far under USD 4,500, or a bypass far under USD 5,000, without a reason, usually means a missing implant, a missing ICU cap, or a hospital you have not verified. A higher figure can be the honest one.
