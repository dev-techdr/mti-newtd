<section id="quick-answer" aria-label="Quick answer">

Fly home after surgery only when the treating clinician says the wound, your breathing, and your clot risk allow it. A typical uncomplicated knee may be two to three weeks after the operation. Heart surgery, transplants, and any complication take longer. The date on a cheap ticket is not a medical clearance. If you are unstable, you stay, even if the stay costs more than the fare you hoped to save.

</section>

## Why is the flight its own medical decision?

A cabin is dry, the air pressure is lower than on the ground, and you sit still for many hours. After surgery those three facts matter. A wound can swell. A lung that is already tired can feel shorter of breath. Blood that is already more likely to clot can clot in a still leg. None of this means you can never fly. It means the surgeon, not the airline sale, picks the day.

TechDr will help change hotels and letters. We will not help you hide a fever so you can board. Airlines can refuse a passenger who looks unfit, and they are right to do so.

## What should you ask before you book the return?

Ask four questions in writing. Is the wound dry? Are you off oxygen and walking the distance the airline will require? Has anyone assessed your clot risk, including a plan for stockings or medicines if they were prescribed? What symptoms mean you must not board? Add a fifth if you had chest or abdominal surgery: when is a pneumothorax or a suture line safe at cabin altitude? The person who operated should answer, or a senior colleague who has seen you.

If the answer is “probably fine”, ask for a date and a review the day before travel. Probably is not a clearance.

## How long do people usually wait?

These are planning ranges for uncomplicated cases, not permission for you. A cataract is often a short stay. A primary knee or hip is often about two to three weeks in India. A bypass is longer, because the breastbone needs protection and the lungs need time. A kidney or liver transplant is longer still, and the team may want you in the city for weeks of blood tests. Cancer surgery depends on the operation. A health check has no wound, but you still should not fly the same day as a sedated endoscopy if you had one.

If there was a return to theatre, a transfusion, a fever, or a clot, the clock restarts. Do not use the uncomplicated range.

## How do you reduce the chance of a clot?

Walk before the flight, during it, and after it. Use the stockings you were given if they fit and were prescribed. Take blood-thinning medicine only if it was prescribed for you. Do not borrow someone else’s tablets. Drink water. Avoid alcohol on the flight. Keep the operated leg moving if the physiotherapist showed you how. A sudden calf swelling, chest pain, or breathlessness is an emergency in the airport as much as in the hospital. Tell the crew. Do not wait for landing because you do not want to make a fuss.

Your companion should know those symptoms too. You may be the last person to judge them clearly.

## What about the wound and pain?

A sealed, dry wound with a dressing plan is the usual requirement. A wound that is opening, draining pus, or surrounded by spreading redness is a reason to stay. Pack the dressings you were given in the cabin bag, not in the hold. You will not retrieve the hold if you need a pad at hour three.

Pain should be controlled with the medicines on your discharge list. Uncontrolled pain is a reason to delay, because you will not walk the cabin, and because it can be a sign of a problem. Keep medicines in original packs with the letter, so airport security and your doctor at home can see what they are.

## What papers do you carry?

Carry the operation note, device labels, medicine list with generic names, allergy list, and the flight clearance in your cabin bag. Carry a copy in email as well. If you are on blood thinners or immunosuppression, the dose and the indication should be on the first page. Your doctor at home should already have an appointment. Landing at night with no plan until “sometime next week” is how early infections get missed.

Insurance documents belong in the same folder. If you bought a policy, know the number to call before you need it.

## What if the clinician says you cannot fly?

Stay. Extend the hotel. Tell the airline, using the medical letter, and ask about a date change. A visa stay limit is a real constraint. If recovery may exceed the days you were granted, raise it with the official channel early rather than overstaying. The extra hotel cost is part of the true cost of surgery abroad. People who budget only the operation line have no room for this, and then they fly anyway. That is the outcome this article is trying to prevent.

If you need oxygen, a stretcher, or a medical escort, that is a commercial airline medical clearance of its own. It takes days and it costs money. It is still cheaper than a crisis at altitude.

## What should happen in the first days at home?

See the doctor you arranged. Check the wound. Check you understand the medicines, especially ones that are dangerous to stop, such as antiplatelets after a stent or immunosuppression after a transplant. Send a photo to the operating team if the discharge letter asked for one. Fever, breathlessness, chest pain, a swollen calf, vomiting that will not stop, or a wound that opens is local emergency care. A video call is not the right tool for those.

Write down the questions you forgot in India. The second conversation is often the useful one.

## How should you plan this before you ever leave home?

Choose return tickets that can be changed. Tell your employer the return date is medical, not fixed. Budget two extra weeks of hotel for major surgery even if you hope not to use them. Travel with a companion who can stay. Read the procedure page for your operation so the usual stay is not a surprise. Then ignore that usual stay if your own recovery is slower.

## What to pack in the cabin bag

Pack the discharge letter, a spare dressing, the prescribed stockings, a list of allergies, and enough medicine for a delay of a day or two. Put a contact card in the same pocket: the hospital’s night number, your companion’s phone, and your doctor at home. Wear loose clothing that does not press on the wound. If you had abdominal or chest surgery, ask whether a small pillow to hold against the wound during turbulence is appropriate. Split medicines so a delayed hold bag cannot separate you from a drug you must not miss, especially blood thinners after a stent or immunosuppression after a transplant.
